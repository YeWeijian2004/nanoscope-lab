01

Why conventional imaging fails

→

The fine stripes exceed the optical cutoff. Their light reaches the camera, but their fine spacing is lost.

The optical limit

Our circular-pupil incoherent model has cutoff 2NA/λ = 4.51 cycles/µm (NA 1.20, λ 532 nm). The sample stripe frequency is 6.52 cycles/µm.

02

What the known pattern does

×

Known bright and dark bands illuminate the fixed sample. Changing phase moves the light, not the specimen.

The illumination model

p = 1 + 0.9 cos(2π k·r + φ). Period ≈285 nm; phases 0°, 120°, 240°; wavevector directions 0° and 90°. Bright bands lie perpendicular to the wavevector.

03

How Moiré patterns appear

Pattern and sample frequencies mix. Broad difference-frequency beats survive the optics and carry clues about the hidden stripes.

Read the beat

6.52 − 3.51 = 3.01 cycles/µm: inside the optical passband. The decorative beat above is schematic; the live camera uses the numerical forward model.

04

Why six captures recover detail

0°120°240°0°120°240°

Three phases separate mixed information. Two directions recover the two stripe orientations. Reconstruction combines the measured clues.

What the solver really does

Saved frames follow y = H[p × s]. The solver receives only captures and known patterns. It minimizes mean ‖H[p × s] − y‖² + 0.0001‖s‖² using up to 80 conjugate-gradient iterations. It never reads ground truth.

This two-direction lesson targets these two stripe regions; it does not give isotropic resolution for arbitrary samples.

Teaching assumptions & sources

Ideal noiseless linear fluorescence, known patterns and optics, periodic boundaries, 128 × 128 pixels over 3.99 µm. No motion, bleaching, aberrations or calibration errors. Negative reconstructed values are retained in calculations and clipped only for display. Specimen images share one linear scale; illumination uses a fixed 0–1.9 scale. This is a teaching model, not a validated quantitative simulation.

FROM SIMULATION TO REAL MICROSCOPY

SIM reveals bacterial division structures

  • Notice the geometry: reconstructed and rotated views expose ring-like FtsZ structures that appear as transverse bands in the conventional view.
  • Known patterns encode detail: illumination shifts fine spatial information into detectable frequencies. Multiple phases and directions help separate those mixed contributions.
  • Computation matters: this published 3D acquisition is more extensive than our six-frame, two-direction stripe lesson. Motion and pattern errors can create reconstruction artefacts.
